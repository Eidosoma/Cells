# **Review and summary: The Evolving Story of How Chemistry Became Biology**

Quanta Magazine · Yasemin Saplakoglu · November 12, 2026

The article explores one of biology's deepest questions: **How did nonliving chemistry become a system capable of maintaining itself, reproducing, inheriting information, and evolving?**

Its central argument is that origin-of-life research is undergoing an important conceptual shift.

For decades, researchers competed over which molecule or process came first: RNA, metabolism, proteins, or cell membranes. Now, increasing attention is going toward understanding **how multiple interacting chemical systems collectively acquired the functions of life.**

## **Executive summary**

The article makes five major points:

1. **Life probably did not begin with a single magic molecule.** It may have emerged through interactions among energy-generating chemistry, replicators, compartments, and information-bearing structures.
2. **The origin of life's building blocks is increasingly understood.** The harder question is how those materials assembled into a persistent, evolving system.
3. **Our last universal common ancestor (LUCA) was already remarkably sophisticated.** This implies that important evolutionary innovations occurred long before the ancestor of all surviving life.
4. **Laboratories are getting closer to building lifelike systems.** Researchers have created molecular assemblies that reproduce, catalyze reactions, form compartments, or divide, although no experiment has yet demonstrated a fully autonomous, independently evolving living system.
5. **Life may be better understood as a continuum of capabilities rather than a binary distinction between living and nonliving matter.** Evolution, reliable inheritance, metabolism, and self-maintenance emerge as central questions.

The deepest implication is that understanding life may require studying the **organization, dynamics, and persistence of interacting systems**, not merely identifying their molecular ingredients.

## **1. The most important scientific results**

**A. Synthetic cells can complete a basic cell cycle**

In July 2026, Kate Adamala's team reported synthetic cells assembled from nonliving components that could replicate DNA, grow, and divide.

Their system uses a roughly 90-kilobase genome, cellular protein-making machinery, membrane-enclosed compartments, and externally supplied nutrients and molecular components.

**Importance:** It demonstrates that several essential cellular functions can be integrated into one engineered system.

**Limitation:** The cells cannot sustain themselves independently, and autonomous evolution was not demonstrated. The work was published as a preprint.

**B. LUCA may have been surprisingly complex**

A 2024 study reconstructed LUCA as a microorganism with approximately 2,600 proteins, a functional metabolism, and cellular machinery comparable in complexity to modern bacteria.

The researchers estimated an age of roughly 4.2 billion years.

**Importance:** If this reconstruction is correct, the transition from primitive chemistry to sophisticated cellular life occurred remarkably early.

**Limitation:** The organism's complexity and age are estimates inferred from surviving genomes, not direct observations.

**C. Chemical replicators can acquire metabolic functions**

Sijbren Otto's group has built self-replicating molecular assemblies that grow and reproduce through a cycle of molecular addition and fragmentation.

Some can catalyze chemical reactions that help generate their own precursors. In 2025, the group also demonstrated natural selection favoring replicators with improved catalytic function.

**Importance:** Inheritance, replication, and primitive metabolism can interact without DNA-based genetics.

**Limitation:** These are not autonomous organisms, and the article's newer claims about dividing compartments were described as unpublished work.

**D. Early proteins may have needed far fewer amino acids**

Klára Hlouchová and colleagues are testing whether proteins can fold using a restricted chemical alphabet.

Experimental and computational results indicate that stable protein structures can be produced using approximately 10 amino-acid types rather than the 20 commonly used by modern biology.

**Importance:** The earliest functional proteins might have been chemically much simpler than today's.

**Limitation:** A stable fold does not by itself demonstrate the full biological function of a protein or establish that ancient life actually used that exact alphabet.

## **2. The deeper theoretical shift**

The most interesting change is not a new molecule or laboratory breakthrough. It is the movement toward studying the transition from chemistry to biology as a **systems problem**.

A simple way to understand the emerging picture is:

**Chemical diversity**

Molecules form, interact, assemble, and break apart

**Self-reinforcing networks**

Reactions help maintain other reactions

**Compartments and energy coupling**

Boundaries concentrate components; environmental energy drives their organization

**Heritable variation**

Systems reproduce while preserving some characteristics and changing others

**Open-ended biological evolution**

Selection improves persistence and creates new capabilities across generations

Conceptual interpretation of the article, not a proven historical sequence. These processes could have appeared together, interacted, or developed in different orders.

The central problem becomes: how can a chemical system preserve enough organization to persist while remaining adaptable enough to evolve?

That question is more general than the identity of RNA, DNA, proteins, or lipids.

## **3. Critical assessment: How convincing is the article?**

This is a strong overview of the field, and the main scientific examples are supported by identifiable research. However, the article's broad narrative is more convincing than some of its implications.

| Claim                                                         | My assessment                                                                           |
| ------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Life may have emerged from interacting chemical networks      | Plausible, increasingly productive research direction, not demonstrated historical fact |
| LUCA lived 4.2 billion years ago                              | Serious published estimate, but dependent on evolutionary models                        |
| Reduced amino-acid alphabets can produce stable protein folds | Supported by substantial experimental and computational work                            |
| Self-replicating chemistry can undergo Darwinian selection    | Demonstrated in selected laboratory chemical systems                                    |
| Scientists have nearly created autonomous life                | Progress is impressive, but critical capabilities remain missing                        |
| Life exists along a continuum                                 | Useful conceptual framework, not yet a universally accepted operational definition      |

There are four important qualifications.

**First, we still don't know the historical sequence.** Showing that life-like properties can emerge through one particular chemistry does not demonstrate that early Earth followed the same route.

**Second, the energy problem deserves more attention.** It is not enough to have chemicals that catalyze each other. Such networks must maintain themselves despite diffusion, degradation, waste accumulation, and environmental fluctuations. Ultimately, a sustained energy source must support that organization.

**Third, inheritance is still an essential missing bridge.** A structure that makes copies of itself isn't necessarily capable of open-ended evolution. It must preserve consequential information, introduce variations, and support selection across generations. The synthetic-cell work and Otto's chemical replicators demonstrate different portions of this problem.

**Fourth, the article is somewhat optimistic about integration.** Researchers have demonstrated many life-like functions separately, but combining them into one robust, self-sustaining, evolving system is substantially harder than placing several mechanisms in the same compartment.

The distinction between laboratory evidence and the actual history of life is fundamental.

## **4. Relevance to Eidosoma and Cells**

I see a particularly strong conceptual connection with your Plastic Heredity research and the multi-scale architecture of Cells.

The article's key theme is that life may be fundamentally about **maintaining and transmitting functional organization**, rather than simply copying particular molecular structures.

That intersects with your work on inherited records, functional renewal, and recovery of organization after perturbation.

However, the article does not establish those more specific hypotheses. The connection is a research opportunity, not external validation.

### **A promising experiment for Cells**

From molecular chemistry toward persistent, evolving systems

| **System**       | Model a small autocatalytic network inside a growing and dividing compartment.                                               |
| ---------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Energy**       | Introduce an environmental energy or chemical gradient required for network maintenance.                                     |
| **Inheritance**  | Allow daughter compartments to inherit molecular compositions or acquired functional states.                                 |
| **Perturbation** | Remove components, alter environmental conditions, or disrupt inherited structure.                                           |
| **Measurement**  | Test whether functional organization reappears across generations, compared with random, scrambled, and no-history controls. |

The most compelling research question would be:

**Can a chemical system evolve the ability to reconstruct its functional organization after damage, even when individual molecular components are replaced?**

An especially useful comparison would be against existing experimental replicator systems, rather than demonstrating the phenomenon only in a synthetic simulation.

Otto's [2025 Nature Catalysis study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41929-025-01409-3) is a strong starting point because it experimentally connects chemical replication, catalytic function, and selection.

Adamala's [2026 synthetic-cell preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.07.01.735724v1.full) is particularly relevant to integrated cellular modeling because it describes explicit component-level mechanisms that might become validation targets in Cells.

## **5. Overall verdict**

Scientific quality: Strong as a synthesis of the field.

Novelty: The integration of recent results and perspectives is more significant than any single new discovery reported in the article.

Uncertainty: High regarding exactly how life originated, despite important experimental advances.

Relevance to Eidosoma: High conceptually, particularly for studying self-maintenance, functional inheritance, and the emergence of adaptive organization.

My biggest takeaway is that the article points toward a potentially powerful meeting of systems chemistry, evolutionary biology, and computational simulation.

One field asks how chemical reactions become self-maintaining. Another asks how those systems become heritable and evolvable. A third asks how functional organization persists despite damage and component replacement.

**The interesting frontier is understanding how all three properties can emerge and reinforce one another in the same physical system.**

That is a compelling area for Cells to investigate, provided the simulations are anchored in experimentally measurable chemistry and tested against alternative mechanisms.
